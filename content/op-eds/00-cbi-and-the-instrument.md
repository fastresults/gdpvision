I have spent seventeen years in and around Caribbean government rooms, and I have watched the same meeting happen more times than I can usefully count. A delegation from a larger country explains, politely, that an arrangement a government depends upon will be ending. Not renegotiated. Ending. Everyone thanks everyone. And in the room afterwards nobody speaks for a while, because every person present is doing the same arithmetic and arriving at the same answer.

Citizenship by Investment is now that arrangement. Five OECS states operate a programme today. At the upper band, receipts reach half of government revenue — a figure drawn from IMF Article IV consultations across those states. Half. Not of a discretionary fund, not of a development budget. Of revenue.

That money is not abstract. It is in hospitals. It is in schools. It is in the reserve a small island reaches for in the weeks after a hurricane, when the roads are gone and the reinsurance has not yet arrived. And it has a scheduled end.

I want to make an argument that is unpopular in some quarters, because I think the region has been arguing about the wrong thing. The phase-out is not the emergency. The absence of a priced, sequenced, financeable replacement is the emergency — and unlike the phase-out itself, that part is entirely within the region's power to fix.

## The arithmetic nobody wants on a slide

Set the dependency against the fiscal position it sits in.

Debt-to-GDP in the upper band of Caribbean sovereigns stands at ninety per cent, on IMF WEO figures. In high-debt cases, interest payments consume around a quarter of government revenue before a Cabinet takes its first discretionary decision of the year.

Now remove up to half of what remains.

There is no version of that arithmetic that resolves through prudence. It cannot be managed by trimming recurrent spending or deferring a capital project. It is a structural hole, and it has to be filled with new economic activity — capital that arrives, converts into output, and generates revenue on a schedule that lines up with the one Brussels has set.

And it has to be done in a hurricane corridor. In 2017, Hurricane Maria cost Dominica 226 per cent of GDP in a single night, on the government's own Post-Disaster Needs Assessment. More than two years of everything the country produces, erased between dinner and dawn. Any transition plan that does not survive that event is not a plan; it is a hope with a spreadsheet attached.

## Why this is not ordinary fiscal pressure

Governments manage revenue shocks all the time. What makes this different is three things, and they compound.

The timetable is set elsewhere. The OECS holds zero votes on the steering committee of the OECD Inclusive Framework — it is on the published governance roster. The rules that reprice the region's access to the global economy are written by people who will never visit, and they arrive as facts. A small state can advocate, and should, but it cannot legislate the deadline away.

The replacement has a lag. Every investment carries a gap between capital arriving and revenue appearing. For a hotel it may be three years. For a regulated financial service, five. For a workforce that does not yet exist, longer. That lag is the entire argument for acting now rather than in the final year of the programme — and it is the variable almost no diversification strategy costs explicitly.

The evidence is late. This is the one that gets least attention and does the most damage. Authoritative sector data reaches a Caribbean Cabinet roughly eighteen months after the period it describes, on a review of Eastern Caribbean Central Bank and national statistical office release cadences. A decision taken today rests on a settled picture of the year before last.

Read those three together and the problem becomes clear. The region is being asked to execute the most consequential economic transition in its modern history, against an external deadline it does not control, using an evidence base that describes a country that no longer exists.

## The plan that is not a plan

Every affected government has a diversification strategy. I have read a number of them. They are articulate documents produced by capable people, and they name the right sectors — agro-processing, digital services, the blue economy, renewable energy, medical tourism.

And almost none of them can answer three questions.

What is the shape of the hole, year by year, under the actual glide-path rather than a hopeful one? Not the total — the annual profile. A gap that opens gradually is a different governing problem from one that lands in a single fiscal year, and it demands a different sequence of decisions.

How much capital does each named sector require, and how much GDP does that capital actually convert into? "Agro-processing" is not a plan. Agro-processing requiring a stated sum of committed capital, converting at a known rate, in a jurisdiction whose land tenure and workforce readiness have been scored — that is a plan. The rest is a list of hopes with sector names attached.

And how long does each replacement take to bite?

The reason these questions go unanswered is not complacency, and in my experience it is usually unfair to suggest otherwise. They go unanswered because the underlying evidence does not exist in a usable form. Ministries hold fragments in incompatible formats. The dependency relationships between sectors live in the heads of a small number of overworked officials, some of them close to retirement.

So when a Cabinet asks what happens if half the revenue goes, the honest answer from the ministry is: give us some months, and we will commission something. The commissioned study arrives after the decision window has closed, describing a situation that has already moved.

That is not a failure of will. It is a failure of instrumentation.

## What the region actually needs

A government that is genuinely prepared for this can do four things on demand — not in six weeks, on demand.

It can show the gap year by year, under a selected glide-path, sourced and graded so the number survives contact with an opposition finance spokesman.

It can show a book of investment packages, each with the capital required, the conversion into GDP, and the honest lag before impact.

It can score its own readiness — legal framework, land, workforce, incentive regime, institutional capacity — and therefore say precisely why a given package is not yet investable and what would make it so. Investors do not walk away because a country is small. They walk away because nobody could tell them whether the land title would clear.

And it can sequence that book across years, because a small state cannot start eleven things at once, and pretending otherwise is how transitions fail.

None of that is exotic. It is ordinary discipline applied to evidence that has been assembled properly. What has been missing is an instrument to hold it.

## Why an instrument, and why now

This is where I should be direct about my own interest, because the reader is entitled to weigh it.

We built GDPVision because we concluded that the region's transition problem is, at its root, an instrumentation problem — and that consultancy alone cannot solve it. A consultancy engagement produces a snapshot that ages from the day it lands, and the institutional knowledge leaves with the team. What a government needs is something it owns and operates: a National Ledger with a twelve-sector ontology, a decade of history, and a confidence grade on every series; a scenario engine that lets a Cabinet rehearse a decision before it costs a cent; and a transition studio that prices the gap and assembles the replacement book with the lags made explicit.

Three commitments matter more than any feature.

Sovereignty is architectural. One isolated deployment per nation, separate database, storage and encryption keys. No cross-instance queries exist in the architecture — not disabled, absent. The government owns its data outright.

Provenance is enforced, not promised. Every figure carries a grade and traces to source. You will notice that every number in this article does the same, including the ones that carry only moderate confidence. An instrument that presents every figure as equally solid is not being confident; it is being careless, and it will eventually be caught.

Nothing releases autonomously. The instrument drafts and prices. Principals decide. There is no path from analysis to public commitment that does not pass through a named human being.

And one thing that is not software at all. A package that exists only in a document does not attract capital. It has to be presented, to real investors, in a room where deals are done. OPEN Interactive has convened the Caribbean Investment Summit since 2009 precisely because that room has to exist and someone has to run it. The strategy document and the channel are not two separate problems; a government that solves only the first has produced a very well-argued PDF.

## The decision already being made

The date has been set by others. That part is finished, and no amount of regional advocacy is likely to move it materially now.

What has not been decided — what remains entirely in the hands of the governments concerned — is whether the replacement is priced, sequenced, readiness-scored and placed in front of capital while there is still time for the lag to run.

That decision is being made right now. It is being made by default, in every month it is not made deliberately. And the cost of default is not a worse outcome in ten years. It is a smaller set of options in two.
