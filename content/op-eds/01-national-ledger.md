A Prime Minister I worked with years ago had a habit I have never forgotten. Before saying any number in public, he would pause — half a second, no more — and you could see him deciding whether he was willing to defend it.

He was right to pause. He had no way of knowing where most of his numbers came from.

This is not a criticism of him, and it is not a criticism of his statisticians, who were serious people doing careful work with inadequate resources. It is a description of a structural condition that almost every small state governs under, and which is so normal that it has stopped being remarked upon.

Authoritative sector data reaches a Caribbean Cabinet roughly eighteen months after the period it describes. That figure comes from a review of Eastern Caribbean Central Bank and national statistical office release cadences, and it carries a moderate confidence grade — which is itself an admission worth making, and one I will return to.

Eighteen months. Consider what that means in practice. A decision taken in mid-2026 rests on a settled picture of late 2024. Between those two points sits a hurricane season, a shift in arrivals, a change in the price of fuel, and possibly an election. The Cabinet is steering by a photograph and being asked to treat it as a window.

## Three separate failures wearing one coat

It is tempting to call this a data problem and reach for the obvious remedy, which is more data. That would be a mistake. There are three distinct failures here and they need different answers.

**The evidence is scattered.** Ministry spreadsheets, central bank bulletins, IMF Article IV consultations, consultancy reports commissioned years apart on incompatible bases, and — I say this without much exaggeration — a certain amount of institutional knowledge that exists only in WhatsApp. There is no single place where a Cabinet can look and no single number that everyone accepts.

**The evidence is stale.** Not because anyone is slow, but because the collection, validation, and publication cycle was designed for statistical record-keeping rather than for governing. Those are different purposes with different tolerances, and we have been asking one system to serve both.

**And the evidence is ungraded.** This is the failure that receives the least attention and does the most damage. A number arrives in a Cabinet paper and looks exactly like every other number in the paper. A figure that is precisely measured and a figure that is a well-intentioned estimate from a survey of uncertain coverage are rendered in identical type. Nothing on the page tells a minister which is which.

The third failure is the one that produces the pause I described. It is what makes a leader hesitate before a microphone. And it is the one nobody talks about, because admitting uncertainty in public is politically expensive in a way that being wrong later, somehow, often is not.

## The cost is a decision, not a report

It would be comfortable to treat this as a technical matter for statisticians. It is not. Poor instrumentation shows up as governing failure in three recognisable forms.

Decisions are taken on figures that later turn out to be wrong, and the correction arrives after the money is committed.

Decisions are *not* taken, because no one in the room can defend a number well enough to justify acting on it — and inaction is always the safest thing to recommend when the evidence is soft.

And when a figure is challenged in public, the government cannot produce its source quickly. What follows is days of ministries phoning one another while the dispute hardens. By the time the answer is assembled, the question has moved on and the doubt has settled.

That third failure is the most corrosive, because it teaches the public that government numbers are negotiable.

## What I mean by a ledger

Fixing this does not require more reports. It requires a different kind of object underneath the reports — something a government owns and operates rather than commissions.

Four properties matter.

**A shared ontology.** One agreed sector structure that everything maps into, with a decade of history behind it, so that a figure from Agriculture and a figure from Tourism can be placed in the same picture without someone reconciling definitions by hand.

**A confidence grade on every series.** This is the important one. Not a claim of accuracy — an honest statement of how much a figure should be trusted. A minister who knows a number is weak can still use it, carefully, with the caveat built into the advice. A minister who does not know is exposed.

**Provenance to source.** Any figure, anywhere, should open to reveal where it came from and how it was derived. The test is simple: when the opposition disputes a number on a Tuesday, can the government produce the source on the Tuesday?

**Continuous refresh.** Not an annual digest. A standing picture that is updated as evidence arrives, so that the eighteen-month lag becomes a much shorter one for the things that move fastest.

## On admitting what we do not know

I noted above that the eighteen-month figure carries only a moderate confidence grade. That was deliberate.

An instrument that presents every number as equally solid is not being confident. It is being careless, and eventually it will be caught. The discipline that actually protects a government is the opposite one: publish the grade alongside the number, in public, and let the reader see which figures are load-bearing and which are indicative.

This is uncomfortable at first. Every communications adviser I have ever worked with has flinched at it. But it is the only version of the practice that survives contact with a hostile press, an IMF mission, or a well-briefed opposition — because it has already conceded the thing they were going to use against you, and conceded it on your terms.

A government that grades its own evidence is not admitting weakness. It is demonstrating that it knows the difference between what it knows and what it estimates.

That distinction is the foundation of every other decision a Cabinet makes. It deserves an instrument, and it deserves rather better than a photograph.
