On the evening of 18 September 2017, Hurricane Maria crossed Dominica. The Post-Disaster Needs Assessment produced afterwards put the damage at 226 per cent of GDP.

Not a bad quarter. Not a downgrade or a missed target. More than two years of everything the country produces, erased between dinner and dawn.

Thirteen years earlier, Hurricane Ivan had done substantially the same thing to Grenada, at 200 per cent of GDP on the OECS and World Bank assessment. And the NOAA record for the past two decades shows Category 4 and 5 Atlantic storms arriving around thirty per cent more frequently than in the preceding baseline.

I set these figures out together not to alarm anyone in the region — nobody in the Caribbean needs to be told about hurricanes — but because of what they imply about how fiscal planning is done here, and how strangely it has continued to be done.

## The plan and the corridor

Every one of these governments produces a medium-term fiscal framework. Those documents are competent. They project revenue, they schedule debt service, they set out capital programmes across three to five years.

And with rare exceptions they are written as though the corridor were not there. The storm appears, if at all, as a contingency line and a paragraph acknowledging vulnerability. The central projection — the numbers that actually drive decisions — assumes a sequence of years in which nothing catastrophic occurs.

For a country where a single event can remove twice its annual output, this is not a conservative assumption. It is a heroic one.

I want to be careful here, because the criticism is easily misdirected. Officials in these ministries know the risk better than any visiting adviser. They live in the corridor. The reason the storm does not appear in the central case is not ignorance. It is that modelling it properly requires capability that does not exist in most of these governments, and commissioning it externally takes months and produces a document rather than an instrument.

So the storm sits in a paragraph, and the plan proceeds as though the paragraph were a plan.

## The question that cannot be answered in time

Consider a real decision. A government is weighing a major capital commitment — a port, an energy transition, a hospital. It has to decide this quarter.

The questions that matter are obvious, and none of them can currently be answered inside the decision window.

What does this do to GDP across five years, on stated assumptions? What does it do to the debt path? What happens to it if a Category 4 storm arrives in year two? Which sectors move when this sector moves — and by how much, given the dependency structure of an economy this concentrated? And how does it compare, on identical assumptions, with the alternative proposal from another ministry?

That last one is where most Cabinets quietly fail. Two proposals arrive, each modelled by different people at different times on different bases, and the comparison is therefore between two incompatible documents. What is actually being compared is the persuasiveness of two ministers.

## Rehearsal is not prediction

The objection I hear most often, usually from the most capable person in the room, is that economic models are always wrong.

They are. I agree with the objection entirely, and it does not survive contact with the purpose.

Nobody serious rehearses a decision in order to know the future. The value of modelling a policy before committing to it lies in four things that have nothing to do with predictive accuracy.

**Comparison under consistent assumptions.** Two options, one basis. The argument becomes about judgement rather than about whose consultant was more convincing.

**Second-order effects.** Small economies are tightly coupled. A tourism decision is a construction decision, a public revenue decision, and — because of the import bill — a balance-of-payments decision. Rehearsal surfaces the propagation. Intuition consistently underestimates it.

**Sensitivity.** Which assumption is the answer fragile to? If the whole case rests on one arrivals number, the Cabinet should know that before it commits, not after.

**Cost, honestly stated.** Every gain is paid for somewhere. A model that reports only the upside is a sales instrument. One that shows what the gain costs elsewhere is a governing instrument, and the difference is not subtle.

And there is a fifth use, which is the one I would argue is most valuable of all and which is almost never available: running the decision backwards. Not "what does this policy do?" but "we need this debt path — which combination of levers reaches it?" That is how a head of government actually thinks. Very little institutional software supports it.

## The season is not a surprise

The uncomfortable observation, and I will put it directly, is that the storm is the most predictable feature of the Caribbean fiscal environment. It arrives every year. Its intensity distribution is documented. Its historical cost is on the record at 226 per cent and 200 per cent of GDP in two well-assessed cases.

The only genuinely unpredictable elements are timing and landfall.

A risk that is certain in kind and uncertain only in date is precisely the kind a government can prepare against — if it can price the consequence in advance. What it cannot do is prepare against a risk it has only described.

Recovery financing negotiated after a storm is negotiated from the weakest position a country ever occupies. Recovery financing arranged against a modelled position, with the fiscal consequence already priced and the sector propagation already understood, is a different conversation with the same lenders.

The difference between those two conversations is worth more than any single capital project in the plan. It costs a fraction as much. And it requires only that the rehearsal happen before the event rather than the assessment happening after it.

Every fiscal plan in this region is written inside a hurricane corridor. It is not unreasonable to ask that they be written as though that were true.
