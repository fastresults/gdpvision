The manifesto is the most carefully written document a party ever produces and the least used document a government ever owns.

It is drafted over months. It is argued over line by line. It is costed, at least notionally. It wins the election. And then, within about a year, it becomes something nobody can quite locate — a PDF on a party website, referred to in speeches, consulted almost never.

I have watched this happen in more administrations than I care to count, on both sides of the aisle, in countries whose politics have nothing in common. It is not a failure of sincerity. Governments do not abandon their manifestos out of cynicism. They lose them structurally, and the mechanism is worth describing precisely, because a problem understood structurally can be fixed structurally.

## How a manifesto is lost

A manifesto makes promises at the level of intention. "We will modernise the health system." "We will make the island competitive for digital investment."

A government delivers at the level of the deliverable — a procurement, a piece of legislation, a training programme, a signed agreement. Between those two levels sits a translation that almost nobody performs formally. Each ministry reads the manifesto, interprets its own portion, and proceeds on that interpretation. Three ministries read the same pledge three different ways, and none of them is wrong, and none of them is checkable.

Eighteen months later the Prime Minister asks which pledges are on track. What returns is a set of ministry self-assessments, written by the people being assessed, in prose rather than in status. Everything is "advancing" or "in train."

Meanwhile the opposition has been keeping a list. Theirs is shorter, sharper, and consists entirely of the things that have not happened. It does not need to be fair. It needs to be first, and it usually is.

## Why this matters more here than elsewhere

In a large economy, a lost manifesto is a political embarrassment. In a small, highly indebted one, it is a fiscal event.

Debt-to-GDP in the upper band of Caribbean sovereigns sits at ninety per cent, on IMF WEO figures. In high-debt cases, interest payments consume a quarter of government revenue before Cabinet decides anything at all.

Read those two numbers together and the consequence is stark. Nine-tenths of annual output is already owed, and a quarter of revenue is spoken for before the first discretionary decision of the year. What remains is small, and every commitment made from it has to land — because there is no second pool to draw on and no margin for a delivery that quietly evaporates.

In that fiscal position, an untracked commitment is not an administrative untidiness. It is money the country did not have to lose.

## The objection, and why I do not accept it

The standard objection to formal manifesto tracking is that manifestos are political documents and governments are constitutional entities, and the two should not be conflated.

There is something in this. In systems where the separation is formal, the tracked document should be the Throne Speech, the budget statement, or the national development plan rather than the party programme. That is a real distinction and it should be respected.

But it is also, frequently, a convenient one. The deeper objection is rarely constitutional. It is that a scored manifesto is uncomfortable, and it is uncomfortable for a reason that deserves to be said plainly: some pledges were written to be vague, and decomposition makes vagueness visible.

To which the honest response is — yes, and that information is worth having privately even if it is never published. A Prime Minister who discovers in month four that a flagship pledge cannot be decomposed into anything measurable has learned something enormously valuable while there is still time to act on it. The same discovery in month forty is a press cycle.

## What a scored mandate actually requires

Three things, none of them exotic.

**Decomposition.** The manifesto broken into pillars, then pledges, then deliverables with a named ministry attached to each. This is the step that gets skipped, and skipping it is why nothing downstream works. It should be done with ministries in the room, not to them.

**A cadence.** Quarterly, because annual is too slow to correct anything and monthly is theatre. Every ministry graded on the same scale, from delivered through to broken. The blunt scale matters — "in train" is not a status, it is an evasion.

**An audit trail.** A signed, versioned compact where every revision is snapshotted and can be compared against what it replaced.

That last element is the one that persuades ministers, and it is worth understanding why. A tracking system that only exposes people will be resisted, sabotaged, and eventually abandoned. One where the promise cannot be quietly rewritten underneath a minister protects them as much as it holds them to account. A ministry that delivered now has proof that it delivered, and proof is a currency inside government that is chronically undersupplied.

## The argument in one sentence

You cannot prevent the scorecard from existing.

A journalist will build one, and it will be built from whatever is publicly visible, which is not the same as what is true. An NGO will build one against its own priorities. The opposition will build one designed to wound. All three will be published, and all three will be discussed as though authoritative.

The only variable within a government's control is whether it has its own — earlier, more granular, built from evidence, and honest enough about the difficult items to be credible on the strong ones.

Governments that have that document walk into their mid-term able to say precisely which ministries are on pace and why. Governments that do not are reduced to disputing someone else's arithmetic, which is a position no incumbent has ever won from.

The manifesto was the most carefully written document your party produced. It is worth more than a drawer.
