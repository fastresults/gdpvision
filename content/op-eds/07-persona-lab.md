In the upper band of Caribbean states, around seventy per cent of citizens with tertiary education have emigrated. The figure is from the World Bank and OECD skilled-migration database, and it carries a high confidence grade.

Seven in ten. The nurses, the teachers, the engineers, the accountants — the people a state spends most heavily on educating and depends on most completely.

Some of the value returns. Remittances reach twenty per cent of GDP in the top-receiving economies. That inflow is real and it is stable, and any honest account has to acknowledge it. But it is also stagnant, exposed to a diaspora that is ageing, and subject to immigration policy set in other countries. It is not a substitute for the people. It is compensation for their absence.

Meanwhile the public-sector nursing vacancy rate across the OECS sits at around eight per cent on PAHO assessments — a shortfall that is felt in a specific hospital on a specific night, by people who are not thinking about migration statistics.

Every government in the region knows this. Every government has a retention policy. And I want to argue that the policies keep failing for a reason that has nothing to do with their content.

## The policy that was never tested

Here is what typically happens.

A retention package is designed by capable officials. It reflects a considered theory of why people leave — usually salary, sometimes housing, occasionally professional development. It is costed, approved, and announced.

And then it does not work as intended, because the theory was wrong in a way nobody had the means to discover.

I have seen a bonded-scholarship scheme fail because the bond itself was read by its intended recipients as an insult rather than an investment. I have seen a housing incentive miss because it was aimed at a life stage the target group had already passed. In neither case was the policy badly designed. In both cases it was designed for a population that existed in the ministry's model rather than in the country.

The failure is not analytical. It is that nobody asked the people it was for, in a form that would have surfaced the objection before the money was committed.

## Why the obvious answer is not available

The obvious answer is research. Ask them first.

For an announced policy, that works and governments do it. For an unannounced one — which is most of the interesting cases — it frequently cannot be done at all, and the reason is worth stating plainly.

The questionnaire reveals the intention. A survey asking nurses how they would respond to a retention bonus tells every respondent, and shortly afterwards the press, that a retention bonus is under consideration. In a country of a hundred thousand people, where the health workforce is a community rather than a demographic, this is not a hypothetical leak. It is a certainty.

So the choice presented to a minister is: announce untested, or test and lose control of the announcement.

Most choose to announce untested. Then the policy lands, the reaction is not what was modelled, and the government spends its second week defending something that could have been adjusted in its second draft.

There is a further constraint that is rarely acknowledged. Proper national survey research is expensive relative to the budgets available, and it is slow relative to the political calendar. A government with a decision due this quarter cannot commission a study that reports next quarter.

## What rehearsal offers, and what it does not

There is now a serious middle path, and I want to describe it carefully — including its limits, because the limits are where credibility is won or lost.

It is possible to model the audiences for a policy from evidence a government already holds: its own corpus, published research, census structure, and the documented positions of the groups concerned. Those models can then be used to rehearse a policy, an incentive, or a message before anything is announced. It is private, it is fast, and it leaks nothing.

Now the limits, stated as plainly as I can.

This does not replace real polling, and any vendor telling a government otherwise should be shown the door. It does not measure public opinion and it cannot be cited as though it did. Modelled audiences are constructed from evidence and inherit every gap in that evidence.

What it does do is genuinely valuable. It surfaces the objection nobody in the ministry anticipated. It shows which of two framings of the same policy fails, and why. It identifies the segment that was left out of the design entirely — in the bonded-scholarship case, the parents, who turned out to be the actual decision-makers. And where a real survey is warranted, it tells you what to ask, which makes the expensive instrument far more productive when you do reach for it.

That is a rehearsal instrument, not a measurement instrument. It should be sold as the first and never as the second.

## The condition I would insist on

Any government adopting this should insist on one thing above all others: every finding traceable back to the evidence it came from.

A modelled finding that cannot be traced is an assertion with a chart attached, and it will eventually be used to justify something it does not support. A finding that opens to reveal its sources can be interrogated by a permanent secretary, challenged by a minister, and defended in public.

The same discipline applies here as everywhere else in government: the value is not the answer, it is the ability to show where the answer came from.

## What is actually at stake

Seven in ten. That number is not a workforce statistic. It is a description of a country slowly losing the capacity to be the thing it is trying to become.

No modelling instrument will fix it. Migration is driven by wages, opportunity, and the pull of larger economies, and those are structural forces that a small state cannot legislate away.

But the region cannot afford to keep spending its narrow fiscal room on retention policies that miss — announced with genuine conviction, and discovered three months later to have been aimed at people who were not there.

Testing before announcing is not a technology question. It is a discipline. The technology has simply, finally, made the discipline affordable for governments this size.
