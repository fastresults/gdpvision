There is a particular silence that follows a certain kind of meeting. I have sat in it more than once. A minister has just been told, politely and by someone from a larger country, that an arrangement their government depends upon will be ending. Not negotiated. Ending. The delegation thanks them for their time. And in the room afterwards, nobody speaks for a while, because everyone is doing the same arithmetic and arriving at the same answer.

Citizenship by Investment is that arrangement. Five OECS states operate a programme today. At the upper band, receipts reach half of government revenue — a figure drawn from IMF Article IV consultations across those five states, and one that should stop any reader who has not seen it before. Half. Not of a discretionary fund. Of revenue.

That money is not abstract. It is in hospitals. It is in schools. It is in the reserve that a small island reaches for in the weeks after a hurricane, when the roads are gone and the reinsurance has not yet arrived. And it now has a scheduled end.

I want to make an argument that will be unpopular in some quarters: the phase-out is not the emergency. The absence of a priced replacement is the emergency, and it is one that regional governments have more control over than they have exercised.

## The plan that is not a plan

Every affected government has a diversification strategy. I have read a number of them. They are, without exception, articulate documents produced by capable people. They name sectors — agro-processing, digital services, the blue economy, renewable energy, medical tourism. They speak of unlocking potential and positioning the nation.

And almost none of them can answer three questions.

First: what is the shape of the hole, year by year, under the actual glide-path rather than a hopeful one? Not the total. The annual profile. A revenue gap that opens gradually is a different governing problem from one that arrives in a single fiscal year, and it demands a different sequence of decisions.

Second: how much capital does each named sector require, and how much GDP does that capital actually convert into? "Agro-processing" is not a plan. Agro-processing requiring US$40m of committed capital, converting at a known rate, in a jurisdiction whose land tenure and workforce readiness have been scored — that is a plan. The rest is a list of hopes with sector names attached.

Third, and this is the one that is almost never asked: how long does each replacement take to bite? Every investment has a lag between capital arriving and revenue appearing. For a hotel it may be three years. For a regulated financial service it may be five. For a workforce that does not yet exist, longer still.

The lag is the entire argument for acting now rather than in the year the revenue goes. A government that begins its transition in the final year of the programme has not begun a transition. It has begun a crisis, with the added disadvantage that everyone can see it.

## Why the analysis has not been done

It would be easy to attribute this to complacency. That would be wrong, and in my experience it is usually unfair.

The analysis has not been done because the underlying evidence does not exist in a usable form. Sector data reaches these Cabinets long after the period it describes. Ministries hold fragments in incompatible formats. The dependency relationships between sectors — what happens to construction when tourism moves, what happens to public revenue when construction moves — live in the heads of a small number of officials, most of whom are overworked and some of whom are close to retirement.

So when a Cabinet asks what happens if half the revenue goes, the honest answer from the ministry is: give us some months, and we will commission something. And the commissioned study arrives after the decision window has closed, describing a situation that has already changed.

This is not a failure of will. It is a failure of instrumentation. And unlike the phase-out itself, it is entirely within the region's power to fix.

## What a serious transition looks like

A government that is genuinely prepared can do four things on demand.

It can show the gap year by year, under a selected glide-path, sourced and graded so that the number survives contact with an opposition finance spokesman.

It can show a book of investment packages, each with the capital required, the conversion into GDP, and the honest lag before impact.

It can score its own readiness — legal framework, land, workforce, incentive regime, institutional capacity — and therefore say precisely why a given package is not yet investable and what would make it so. Investors do not walk away because a country is small. They walk away because nobody could tell them whether the land title would clear.

And it can sequence the book across years, because a small state cannot start eleven things at once and pretending otherwise is how transitions fail.

None of that is exotic. It is ordinary discipline, applied to evidence that has been assembled properly. What has been missing is the instrument to hold it.

## The part that is not about software

I have spent seventeen years in and around these rooms, and I will end on the thing that matters more than any analysis.

A package that exists only in a document does not attract capital. It has to be presented, to real investors, in a room where deals are done. OPEN Interactive has convened the Caribbean Investment Summit since 2009 precisely because that room has to exist and someone has to run it.

The strategy document and the channel are not two separate problems. A government that solves only the first has produced a very well-argued PDF.

The date has been set by others. That part is finished, and no amount of regional advocacy is likely to move it materially. What has not been decided — what is still entirely in the hands of the five governments concerned — is whether the replacement is priced, sequenced, and placed in front of capital while there is still time for the lag to run.

That decision is being made right now, by default, in every month it is not made deliberately.
