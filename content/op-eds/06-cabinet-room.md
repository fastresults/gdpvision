Ask a Cabinet Secretary what was decided at the meeting three months ago, who owned it, and whether it happened.

You will get an answer about the first. Minutes exist and they are usually well kept. The second becomes vaguer. The third is often genuinely unknown, and the honest ones will say so.

This is not a Caribbean problem. It is close to universal, and I have found it in governments of every size and sophistication. But it is far more expensive in a small, highly indebted state, and that is the case I want to make.

## The arithmetic of a narrow room

Debt-to-GDP in the upper band of Caribbean sovereigns stands at ninety per cent on IMF WEO figures. In high-debt cases, interest payments consume around a quarter of government revenue.

Put those together. Before a Cabinet takes its first discretionary decision of the year, a quarter of what it has to work with is already committed to servicing what was borrowed before. Nine-tenths of annual output is owed.

What remains is genuinely small. And in a room that small, a decision that is taken and then quietly forgotten is not an administrative untidiness. It is a use of the country's scarcest resource that produced nothing.

I have seen this precisely: an infrastructure commitment made in one Cabinet, allocated, and then — through no bad faith, simply through the absence of any mechanism for following it — not executed, not cancelled, and not noticed for eleven months. The money was neither spent nor released. It sat.

## Three failures, none of them anyone's fault

**Minutes are the wrong instrument.** They are a record of proceedings. They capture that a matter was discussed and a direction agreed. They do not capture, in a form anyone can query, that Ministry X owns deliverable Y by date Z against KPI W. The information required to follow a decision is different in kind from the information a minute is designed to hold.

**Ownership dissolves at the door.** A Cabinet decision is collective. Delivery is not — it is always a specific ministry, and usually a specific official within it. That translation happens informally or not at all. Everyone leaves the room believing someone is handling it.

**Cabinet time is spent on presentation rather than decision.** Because there is no shared evidence base, a large part of every session is consumed by ministers establishing facts that ought to have been settled beforehand. Two proposals arrive modelled on different bases, so the comparison is between two documents rather than two options. The scarcest hours in government go on reconciliation.

## What a session could look like

I want to describe a Cabinet session as it could reasonably be run, because I think the gap between this and current practice is smaller than it appears.

Ministers arrive to a situation board — the state of the nation as of this morning, drawn from a ledger everyone in the room already accepts. There is no argument about the underlying numbers, because that argument was settled upstream where it belongs.

The agenda consists of options that have already been rehearsed. Each was modelled before the meeting, on the same assumptions, with its assumptions visible. Two proposals can be placed side by side and genuinely compared, because they were built on the same basis.

A decision is taken. It is recorded in the room, at the moment it is made, with a named owner and a date. Not written up afterwards by someone reconstructing intent from notes — recorded live, while the people who decided it are present to confirm the wording.

And in the following weeks the commitment is visible. What was adopted, who owns it, where it stands. Not requested. Not chased. Visible.

Then, at the next session, the scorecard shows movement since the last one against the same ratified indicators. The Cabinet can see whether the things it decided are moving.

None of this is technologically remarkable. What it requires is that the evidence exist beforehand and the record exist afterwards. Both are absent today, and their absence is why the room works the way it does.

## The objections worth taking seriously

**"Cabinet proceedings are confidential."** They are, and they must remain so. But the current method is usually email, shared drives, and paper — and confidentiality by informality is not confidentiality. An isolated national deployment with visibility enforced in the database and an immutable audit log is materially more confidential than what most governments do now. I would make this argument to any security adviser and expect to win it.

**"Ministers will resist being tracked."** Some will, and the resistance is political rather than technical, so it has to be handled politically. Two things help. The commitments belong to the Cabinet collectively, not to individuals — the framing is delivery, not surveillance. And a ministry that delivered now has proof that it delivered. Proof is chronically undersupplied inside government, and ministers respond to that once they see it.

**"We already have minutes."** Yes. Minutes are necessary. They are simply not sufficient, and no amount of improving them turns them into a delivery record.

## Why this outlasts a government

There is a fear that goes unspoken in every conversation of this kind: that an incoming administration will tear the whole thing out.

The answer is that a decision record is politically neutral in a way almost nothing else in government is. It does not editorialise. It states what was decided, by whom, and what happened. That is as useful to an incoming government wanting to know what it has inherited as it is to the one that built it — arguably more so.

Institutional memory is the thing small states lose most easily and can least afford to lose. Four people leaving can take a subject with them.

A Cabinet that records its decisions properly is not building an administrative convenience. It is building the one asset that survives an election.

And in a fiscal position this tight, it is also simply the difference between deciding something and having decided something.
