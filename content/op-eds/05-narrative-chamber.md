The Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States holds zero votes on the steering committee of the OECD Inclusive Framework.

Zero. It is on the published governance roster for anyone who wishes to check.

That body has overseen the implementation of a fifteen per cent global minimum corporate tax, now in force. Over the past five years, four Caribbean jurisdictions have appeared on active EU tax or anti-money-laundering lists. Correspondent banks have withdrawn from the region on their own commercial timetable, informing governments rather than consulting them.

I have watched ministers receive each of these developments. The pattern does not vary. The decision has already been taken. The delegation is courteous. The timetable is not negotiable. And the government of a state with a population smaller than a mid-sized European town is expected to absorb a structural change to its economy that it had no part in designing.

This essay is not an argument about the fairness of that. Fairness has been argued at length by better-placed people than me and has moved very little. I want to make a narrower and more useful argument about the part that remains within a small state's control.

## The seventy-two hours

When one of these decisions lands, a specific sequence begins.

There is a wire story. Within hours there is regional coverage. Within a day there is domestic political reaction, usually from an opposition that does not need to be accurate, only fast. Within two days investors, lenders, and rating analysts have formed a view — and views formed early are revised slowly.

Meanwhile, inside the government, the communications team is doing something that will be familiar to anyone who has been in one of these buildings. They are starting from a blank page. They are telephoning three ministries for facts. They are waiting for a Ministry of Finance official to return from a meeting because he is the only person who knows what the actual exposure is.

A defensible position emerges on the Thursday. By then the position has hardened without it.

And the cost is not political vanity. It is economic. Investor confidence, currency sentiment, and the tone of the next credit review all move on narrative before they move on fundamentals. A government that is consistently three days late to its own story pays for that lateness in terms it can measure.

## Why the delay is structural

The instinct is to blame the communications team. In every case I have examined closely, this has been wrong.

They are late because they are starting from nothing, every time.

Whatever was learned during the last EU listing is not written down anywhere reachable. The analysis of what the previous correspondent withdrawal actually cost lives in a consultant's report that nobody can now locate. The three officials who understood the tax exposure best have moved on, retired, or gone to Washington. Every crisis begins as though it were the first.

Institutional memory in a small state is unusually fragile, because the institution is unusually small. Losing four people can mean losing a subject.

So the delay is not a personnel problem or a talent problem. It is an architecture problem, and it will recur at exactly the same length every time until the architecture changes.

## What preparation actually means

Being prepared for an external shock does not mean predicting which one arrives. It means three things.

**Standing awareness.** Knowing what is being said now, not last week — across regional and international press, and across the material the opposition is publishing. Not surveillance of citizens; monitoring of the public record, which is a normal function of any serious foreign ministry and which most small states cannot currently perform at scale.

**A corpus that persists.** Every dossier, every source, every position taken, retained and organised so that the next event on the same subject begins from what was learned during the last one. This is the single highest-value thing a small government can build, and it compounds. The third crisis is where a team that has been accumulating becomes structurally faster than one that has not, regardless of how able either team is.

**Positions cited to source.** A defensible line is one where every claim traces to evidence a hostile journalist can be shown. Assertion is not defence. Assertion is what gets picked apart on the second day.

Do those three things and the seventy-two hours becomes a working day. That is not a marginal improvement. It is the difference between shaping the interpretation of an event and inheriting someone else's.

## The line I will not cross

There is a version of this argument that ends somewhere I want to explicitly reject.

It is now technically possible to build systems that detect a signal, draft a response, and publish it without a human being involved. Some vendors will offer governments exactly that, and they will describe the absence of human delay as a feature.

No serious state should install it, and I would not build it.

The principle we hold to is simple and it is not negotiable: the instrument drafts, principals decide, and nothing releases autonomously. There is no path from a detected signal to a public statement that does not pass through a named human being who is accountable for it.

This is partly about risk. A system that speaks for a state will eventually speak wrongly for a state, and the recovery from that is worse than any delay it saved.

But it is mainly about what a government is. The words a state says in public are an act of sovereignty. Automating them is not efficiency. It is a transfer of something that should not be transferred — least of all by a small state that has spent a decade watching other people make its decisions.

The OECS holds zero seats. That number is unlikely to change soon, and pretending otherwise helps nobody.

What can change is whether these governments arrive at the argument with their evidence assembled, their position cited, and their answer ready on the first day rather than the fourth.

That is not the same as having a seat. It is, however, entirely within reach — and it is the difference between being repriced and being negotiated with.
