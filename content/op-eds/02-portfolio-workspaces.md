Ask any minister in the region what their portfolio contributes to national GDP.

In most governments, that question begins a procurement. A consultant is engaged. Some weeks later a figure returns, and the Ministry of Finance disputes the methodology. The minister, who wanted a number for a Cabinet paper due on Thursday, has spent money to acquire an argument.

I have watched this cycle enough times to be confident it is the norm rather than the exception, and it produces a specific kind of government: one in which ministers advocate for their portfolios without knowing what those portfolios are worth.

## The concentration problem

Set that against the structure of these economies.

Direct and indirect tourism contribution reaches eighty per cent of GDP in the upper band of Caribbean states, on WTTC figures. Across the region, a single source market — the United States — dominates stopover arrivals.

Read that plainly. In the most concentrated cases, one sector is not part of the economy. It very nearly is the economy, and it is priced by decisions taken in another country.

Two consequences follow, and both are governing problems rather than economic ones.

The Minister of Tourism holds a portfolio of extraordinary systemic weight and frequently cannot demonstrate it with evidence to hand. When arrivals move, the effect propagates into construction, transport, public revenue, and the import bill — but the propagation is understood anecdotally, by people who have watched it happen, rather than quantified in a form that can be put in front of Cabinet.

And every other minister is arguing for budget against a colleague whose sector is the economy, without being able to state what their own portfolio contributes or which of their available levers moves the national number. Agriculture, the blue economy, the digital sector — each may be strategically vital and each is asked to compete for allocation using adjectives.

## What ministers are actually denied

It is worth being precise about what is missing, because "better data for ministries" is the kind of phrase that has appeared in every public sector reform document for thirty years and delivered very little.

A minister needs four things, and none of them is a report.

**Their contribution.** The portfolio's share of national output and of national exposure, on the same basis as every other portfolio, so the comparison is meaningful. Not a bespoke study — a standing figure.

**The dependency web.** What this sector depends on and what depends on it. In economies this tightly coupled, second-order effects frequently exceed first-order ones, and they are consistently underestimated by intuition. A Minister of Agriculture who can show what a change in her sector does to the import bill is making a fiscal argument, not an agricultural one.

**Their levers, with consequences attached.** Which instruments actually available to this ministry move the number, by how much, and at what cost elsewhere. Most ministers can list their powers. Very few can rank them by effect.

**A shelf of their own scenarios.** Proposals in progress, from early draft through to Cabinet-adopted, held in one place with their assumptions intact — so that a proposal declined in March can be revisited in September without being rebuilt from nothing.

## The reconciliation dividend

There is a benefit here that governments consistently underestimate, and it has nothing to do with any individual ministry.

When every portfolio reads from the same ledger, inter-ministerial argument changes character. Today a great deal of Cabinet time is consumed by ministries disputing each other's underlying figures — not because anyone is acting in bad faith, but because each has built its picture from different sources on different bases at different times. All of them are defensible. None of them agree.

Once the evidence base is shared, that argument moves upstream to where it belongs — to the stewards responsible for data quality — and Cabinet time is freed for the argument that actually requires ministers, which is about judgement and priority.

I would put it more strongly. In small states, the volume of senior time lost to reconciling incompatible numbers is one of the largest hidden costs in government, and it is almost never measured because nobody has ever been asked to account for it.

## The objection from the ministries

The obvious objection is that making contribution visible will be used to punish ministries that contribute little.

It is a serious objection and it deserves a serious answer rather than a reassurance.

First, on framing: the useful measure is contribution *and levers*, not contribution alone. A ministry with modest direct output but powerful levers over a large sector is doing important work, and a system that reports only the first number will misrepresent it. Any implementation that omits the levers deserves the resistance it will get.

Second, on separation: scoring ministries against commitments is a legitimate exercise, but it is a different exercise, with a different consent, and it should not be smuggled in under the heading of portfolio visibility. Conflating the two is the fastest way to lose the ministries entirely — and once lost, they are not easily recovered.

Third, and most practically: ministers respond to this once they see what it does for them. A minister who can walk into Cabinet with their contribution, their dependency web, and their ranked levers on a screen is in a materially stronger position than one with a well-written memorandum. That is not surveillance. That is a better weapon in a fair fight.

## The unit of use

One last observation, and it is the thing most systems of this kind get wrong.

Ministers in small states do not sit at desks. They are at sites, at openings, in vehicles between engagements, and abroad. The realistic unit of use is ninety seconds on a telephone between two meetings — not an afternoon with a dashboard.

Anything built for these users that assumes a desk will be opened twice and abandoned. Anything that answers one question well in ninety seconds becomes part of how they work.

That is not a minor design consideration. It is the whole difference between a system a government bought and a system a government uses.

Ask a minister what their portfolio contributes. It is a reasonable question. It ought to have a reasonable answer, available on the day it is asked, and not from a consultant.
